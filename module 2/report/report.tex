\section{Introduction}

This report presents the solutions to the exercises of Laboratory~2 of the Performance Evaluation and Dimensioning of Networks and Systems (DDRS) course, whose aim is to study discrete-time Markov chains (DTMCs), continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs), queuing systems, and their applications. The exercises are organized in the same blocks as the laboratory guide: discrete-time Markov chains (Section~\ref{sec:A}), DTMCs and CTMCs (Section~\ref{sec:B}), queuing systems (Section~\ref{sec:C}), workloads (Section~\ref{sec:D}), and server farms (Section~\ref{sec:E}).

Each exercise follows the objective--approach--results--conclusion structure used in the Laboratory~1 report. The analytical results are computed in R and compared with discrete-event simulation; following the work guidelines, simulation results are presented with 95\% confidence intervals.

\section{A. Discrete-time Markov chains}\label{sec:A}

\subsection{Exercise 1: Bernoulli and 2-DTMC sample paths}

\textbf{Objective:} Compare Bernoulli processes with two-state discrete-time
Markov chains (2-DTMCs) using parameter pairs that have the same stationary mean
but markedly different temporal behavior, and identify what a 2-DTMC can model
that a Bernoulli process cannot.

\textbf{Approach:} The script \texttt{dtmc\_bernoulli\_plot.R} generates 200
samples of a 2-DTMC with transition matrix
\[
P =
\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha & 1-\alpha \\
\beta & 1-\beta
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where $\alpha$ is the probability of staying in state~0 and $\beta$ the
probability of moving from state~1 to state~0, together with 200 samples of a
Bernoulli process whose success probability equals the stationary probability
of state~1,
\[
\pi_1 = \frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha+\beta}.
\]
We used $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.9,0.1)$ and $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.1,0.9)$. Both give
$\pi_1=0.5$, so the two chains, and the Bernoulli process they are compared with,
have the same fraction of 1s; they differ only in their temporal behavior. This
can be quantified by two properties. In a 2-DTMC, the number of consecutive slots
spent in a state (a \emph{run}) is geometric, with mean $1/(1-\alpha)$ in
state~0 and $1/\beta$ in state~1. The second eigenvalue of $P$ is $\alpha-\beta$,
so the autocorrelation at lag~$k$ is $(\alpha-\beta)^k$. For a Bernoulli process
with $p=0.5$, the mean run length is~2 and the samples are uncorrelated
(Table~\ref{tab:dtmc-bern}).

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Run lengths and correlation of the processes compared ($\pi_1=0.5$ in all cases).}
    \label{tab:dtmc-bern}
    \begin{tabular}{l|cc|c}
        Process & Mean run, state 0 & Mean run, state 1 & Lag-1 autocorr. \\
        \hline
        Bernoulli, $p=0.5$                 & 2    & 2    & 0      \\
        2-DTMC, $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.9,0.1)$ & 10   & 10   & $+0.8$ \\
        2-DTMC, $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.1,0.9)$ & 1.11 & 1.11 & $-0.8$ \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

    \end{subfigure}

\end{figure}

\textbf{Results:} With $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.9,0.1)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:dtmc-a09}),
both states are ``sticky'': the chain stays in a state for 10 slots on
average, and the sample path shows runs of 1s lasting up to about 40 slots,
while the runs of the Bernoulli process rarely exceed a few slots. With
$(\alpha,\beta)=(0.1,0.9)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:dtmc-a01}), the opposite happens:
the chain changes state in 90\% of the slots, so the path is an almost regular
alternation $0,1,0,1,\dots$, which is much more regular than the
Bernoulli process. In both cases the Bernoulli process looks the same, since it
only depends on $\pi_1=0.5$.

\textbf{Conclusion:} The fraction of 1s alone cannot distinguish these three
processes. A Bernoulli process is memoryless: each sample is independent of the
previous one, so its single parameter fixes both the mean and the run lengths.
A 2-DTMC has two parameters, so it can set the mean and the correlation between
consecutive samples independently. It can therefore model \emph{bursty}
behavior (positive correlation, long runs), such as clustered attacks,
burst errors on a wireless channel (the Gilbert--Elliott model), or ON/OFF
traffic sources, as well as \emph{alternating} behavior (negative correlation),
neither of which a Bernoulli process can represent.

\subsection{Exercise 2: Estimating transitions from the attack trace}

\textbf{Objective:} Estimate the transition probabilities of a 2-DTMC from the sequence of daily server attack indicators in \texttt{2dtmcdata.txt}.

\textbf{Approach:} The script \texttt{E2\_AttackDTMC.R} imports the supplied file with the tab separator specified in the exercise. It counts each consecutive transition $i\to j$ and divides by the number of transitions originating in state~$i$. The trace contains 2,000 observations and thus 1,999 transitions.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Estimated transition counts and probabilities for the attack trace.}
    \label{tab:attack-transitions}
    \begin{tabular}{c|rr|rr}
        & \multicolumn{2}{c|}{Transition counts} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Estimated probabilities} \\
        Current state & To 0 & To 1 & To 0 & To 1 \\
        \hline
        0 (not attacked) & 623 & 577 & 0.5192 & 0.4808 \\
        1 (attacked) & 577 & 222 & 0.7222 & 0.2778 \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Results and conclusion:} The estimated transition matrix, with rows denoting the current state, is
\[
\widehat{P} =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5192 & 0.4808 \\
0.7222 & 0.2778
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The fitted chain estimates an attack probability of approximately $0.3997$ in steady state. An attack is followed by another attack with estimated probability $0.2778$; otherwise, the process returns to the non-attacked state with probability $0.7222$. These are empirical estimates from this finite trace, rather than known properties of all server attacks.

\subsection{Exercise 3: Slotted ALOHA}

\textbf{Objective:} Study the theoretical and simulated throughput of slotted ALOHA with a finite number of users, explain the effect of the parameters $N$, $\sigma$, and $p$, and compare ALOHA with carrier-sensing protocols.

\subsubsection{a. Theoretical throughput}

\textbf{Approach:} The theoretical throughput is computed by the function \texttt{aloha\_theo()} and plotted versus $\sigma$ by the supplied script \texttt{aloha\_plot.R}. With $N=10$, it plots four curves for $p$: 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, and 0.6. With $N=25$, it plots $p=0.2$ and $p=0.3$. In both plots, $\sigma$ is logarithmically spaced as specified in the script.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../images/aloha10.jpeg}
    \caption{Theoretical slotted-ALOHA throughput for $N=10$ and the four requested values of $p$.}
    \label{fig:aloha-n10}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../images/aloha25.jpeg}
    \caption{Theoretical slotted-ALOHA throughput for $N=25$ and the two requested values of $p$.}
    \label{fig:aloha-n25}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Results and conclusion:} Increasing $\sigma$ makes more THINKING users transmit. Throughput can first increase as successful attempts become more frequent, then decrease when the added attempts create more collisions and BACKLOGGED users. Increasing $p$ makes BACKLOGGED users retry more often; this can clear a small backlog, but can also increase collisions when many users are waiting. Increasing $N$ adds potential transmissions and contention, so throughput depends on the interaction between $N$, $p$, and $\sigma$, rather than increasing monotonically with any one parameter.

\subsubsection{b. Discrete-event simulation}

\textbf{Approach:} Users are statistically identical, so the state is only
the backlog size $n$. In each slot, $T\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N-n,\sigma)$ THINKING
and $B\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$ BACKLOGGED users transmit. If $T+B=1$ the slot is
a success ($n\to n-1$ if $B=1$); otherwise $n\to n+T$, since colliding
THINKING users become BACKLOGGED. Throughput is the fraction of successful
slots. We ran $R=20$ independent replications of $2\times10^5$ slots
(after $10^4$ warm-up slots) and computed 95\% Student-$t$ confidence
intervals. The theoretical values come from \texttt{aloha\_theo()}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Theoretical and simulated throughput ($N=10$; simulation: mean $\pm$ 95\% CI half-width).}
    \label{tab:aloha-comparison}
    \begin{tabular}{rrr|rcrc}
        $N$ & $\sigma$ & $p$ & Theory & Simulation & Rel.\ err.\ (\%) & Theory in CI? \\
        \hline
        10 & 0.001 & 0.3 & 0.01000 & $0.01001 \pm 0.00011$ & $+0.09$ & yes \\
        10 & 0.050 & 0.3 & 0.31541 & $0.31608 \pm 0.00125$ & $+0.21$ & yes \\
        10 & 0.100 & 0.3 & 0.16415 & $0.16445 \pm 0.00040$ & $+0.18$ & yes \\
        10 & 0.100 & 0.6 & 0.00160 & $0.00158 \pm 0.00004$ & $-1.10$ & yes \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Conclusion:} The theoretical value lies inside the confidence interval
in all four cases. The relative errors are below 0.3\% for the first three
cases. The last one is about $-1.1\%$, which is within the CI (its half-width is
about 2\% of the mean) because the throughput is so low that few successes
are observed. The four operating points span two orders of magnitude, from
light load ($\approx N\sigma$) to a collapsed system where most users are
backlogged. Raising $\sigma$ from 0.05 to 0.1 halves the throughput, and raising
$p$ from 0.3 to 0.6 at $\sigma=0.1$ reduces it by a factor of about 100.

\subsubsection{c. ALOHA vs.\ CSMA, CSMA/CD and CSMA/CA}

\textbf{Approach:} Random-access throughput is limited by collisions. The
protocols differ in (i) how long a frame is exposed to collisions (the
\emph{vulnerable period}) and (ii) how much channel time a collision wastes.
Let $T$ be the frame transmission time, $\tau$ the propagation delay and
$a=\tau/T$.

    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}
    \begin{axis}[
        xmode=log, domain=0.01:30, samples=200,
        width=0.8\textwidth, height=6.5cm, grid=both,

        ylabel={Throughput $S$}, ymin=0, ymax=1,

        \addplot[thick,red]{x*exp(-x)};
        \addplot[thick,black]{x*exp(-0.01*x)/(x*1.02+exp(-0.01*x))};
        \legend{Pure ALOHA, Slotted ALOHA, Non-persistent CSMA ($a=0.01$)}
    \end{axis}
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{Classical throughput curves (infinite population).}
    \label{fig:mac-throughput}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering \small
    \caption{Random-access protocols compared.}
    \label{tab:mac}
    \begin{tabular}{p{1.9cm}p{4.8cm}p{4.4cm}p{3.4cm}}
        \hline
        Protocol & How it works & Throughput & Used in \\
        \hline
        Pure ALOHA & Transmit immediately; no ACK $\Rightarrow$ retry after a random delay.
          & Vulnerable period $2T$. $S_{\max}=1/(2e)\approx0.18$.
          & ALOHAnet, LoRaWAN uplink \\
        Slotted ALOHA & Same, but only at slot boundaries.
          & Vulnerable period $T$. $S_{\max}=1/e\approx0.37$.
          & RFID (EPC Gen2), cellular/satellite random-access channels \\
        CSMA & ``Listen before talk'': if the channel is busy, defer
          (1-persistent: send when idle; non-persistent: wait a random time and re-sense;
          $p$-persistent: send with probability $p$ per slot).
          & Vulnerable period shrinks to $\tau$. For $a=0.01$, non-persistent reaches $\approx0.8$; falls as $a$ grows.
          & Early packet radio; basis of the two variants below \\
        CSMA/CD & CSMA plus listening \emph{while} sending. On a collision: abort,
          send a jam signal, wait a random time (binary exponential backoff).
          Needs frame time $\ge 2\tau$.
          & A collision wastes $\approx2\tau$ instead of $T$. Efficiency $\approx 1/(1+5a)$ (approx.).
          & Shared Ethernet (IEEE 802.3, hubs/coax); not in switched full-duplex \\
        CSMA/CA & Wait for the channel to be idle (DIFS), then count down a random
          backoff, frozen while busy; receiver ACKs; optional RTS/CTS.
          Avoids collisions since a radio cannot detect them while sending.
          & Fewer collisions than CSMA, but backoff and ACK overhead lower the gain.
          & IEEE 802.11 Wi-Fi, IEEE 802.15.4 (Zigbee) \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Conclusion:} Each step wastes less channel time. Slotting halves the
vulnerable period ($2T\to T$) and doubles the peak throughput. Carrier sensing
cuts it to about $\tau$, so for $a\ll1$ throughput exceeds $0.8$. Collision
detection reduces the cost of a collision from a full frame to about $2\tau$.
CSMA/CA gives up part of this gain to work where collisions cannot be detected
(wireless). When $a$ is large (e.g.\ satellite links), sensing is of little
help and ALOHA-type access remains in use. The finite-population model of
Exercises~3a and~3b shows the same qualitative behavior as the
infinite-population curves: throughput first grows with the transmission
probability, peaks at an intermediate load, and then collapses as more users
become backlogged (Figures~\ref{fig:aloha-n10}--\ref{fig:aloha-n25} and
Table~\ref{tab:aloha-comparison}).

\textbf{References:} Bertsekas \& Gallager, \emph{Data Networks}, Ch.~4;
Rom \& Sidi, \emph{Multiple Access Protocols}; Kleinrock \& Tobagi (1975);
Kurose \& Ross, \emph{Computer Networking}.

\section{B. Discrete-time Markov chains and Continuous-time Markov chains}\label{sec:B}

\subsection{Exercise 4: Limiting probabilities of a DTMC}

\textbf{Approach:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(i) Balance equations:} solve $\pi P=\pi$, $\sum_i\pi_i=1$ as
    the linear system $(P^{T}-I)\pi=0$, with its last equation replaced by
    $\sum_i\pi_i=1$ (\texttt{solve()}).
    \item \textbf{(ii) Matrix power:} compute $P^{n}$ with \texttt{expm}
    (\texttt{P \%\^{}\% n}). For large $n$ all rows converge to $\pi$.
    \item \textbf{(iii) Simulation:} starting in state 0, each step draws the
    next state with \texttt{sample()} using the row of $P$ of the current
    state. $\pi_i$ is the fraction of time in state $i$ after a warm-up of
    $10^3$ steps; we ran 30 independent runs of $10^5$ steps and computed
    95\% Student-$t$ confidence intervals.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Results:}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../images/E4_convergence.jpeg}
    \caption{First row of $P^n$ versus $n$. Dotted lines: solution of the balance equations.}
    \label{fig:dtmc-conv}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Limiting state probabilities (simulation: mean $\pm$ 95\% CI half-width).}
    \label{tab:dtmc-limiting}
    \begin{tabular}{l|ccc}
        Method & $\pi_0$ & $\pi_1$ & $\pi_2$ \\
        \hline
        Balance equations     & 0.2593 & 0.1481 & 0.5926 \\
        Matrix power ($n=50$) & 0.2593 & 0.1481 & 0.5926 \\
        Simulation            & $0.2597\pm0.0007$ & $0.1479\pm0.0002$ & $0.5924\pm0.0008$ \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Conclusion:} The three methods agree. The balance equations give
$\pi_1=\tfrac47\pi_0$ and $\pi_2=4\pi_1$, hence $\pi=(7,4,16)/27$. The rows of
$P^n$ converge to $\pi$ after about 10 steps (Figure~\ref{fig:dtmc-conv}),
because the second-largest eigenvalue modulus of $P$ is $\approx0.37$, so the
error shrinks as $0.37^n$; at $n=50$ all rows equal $\pi$ to within $10^{-15}$.
The simulation estimates are within 0.2\% of the exact values. The confidence
intervals of $\pi_0$ and $\pi_2$ contain the exact values, while that of
$\pi_1$ narrowly misses it. This is expected: with three 95\% intervals, one
miss is not unusual, and the discrepancy is negligible in practice.
State~2 dominates ($\approx59\%$) because it is easy to enter (from states 0
and 1) and hard to leave (self-loop 0.8), while state~1 is the rarest: it is
only entered from state~2 with probability 0.2.

\subsection{Exercise 5: Simulation of a CTMC}

\textbf{Approach:} The chain of Figure~2 has generator matrix
\[
Q=\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 & 0\\ 1 & -2 & 1\\ 1 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
\emph{Theory:} the limiting probabilities solve $\pi Q=0$, $\sum_i\pi_i=1$,
as a linear system in which the last equation is replaced by $\sum_i\pi_i=1$
(\texttt{solve()}). \emph{Simulation:} the CTMC was simulated in two equivalent ways.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{View 1:} the chain stays in state $i$ for an exponential time
    with rate $q_i=\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda_{ij}$, then jumps to state $j$ with
    probability $\lambda_{ij}/q_i$.
    \item \textbf{View 2:} each possible transition $i\to j$ has an independent
    exponential timer with rate $\lambda_{ij}$; the smallest timer fires, and
    the chain moves to the corresponding state.
\end{itemize}
In both cases $\pi_i$ is estimated as the fraction of time spent in state $i$.
We ran 30 independent runs of $10^5$ jumps each (after $10^3$ warm-up jumps),
starting in state~1, and computed 95\% Student-$t$ confidence intervals.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{../images/E5_paths.jpeg}
    \caption{Sample paths of the CTMC generated with each view (first 40 jumps).}
    \label{fig:ctmc-paths}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Limiting state probabilities (simulation: mean $\pm$ 95\% CI half-width).}
    \label{tab:ctmc-limiting}
    \begin{tabular}{l|ccc}
        Method & $\pi_1$ & $\pi_2$ & $\pi_3$ \\
        \hline
        Theory ($\pi Q=0$) & 0.3333 & 0.4444 & 0.2222 \\
        Simulation, view 1 & $0.3333\pm0.0007$ & $0.4444\pm0.0006$ & $0.2222\pm0.0006$ \\
        Simulation, view 2 & $0.3333\pm0.0007$ & $0.4446\pm0.0005$ & $0.2220\pm0.0007$ \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Conclusion:} Solving $\pi Q=0$ gives $\pi_3=\pi_2/2$ and
$\pi_1=\tfrac32\pi_3$, hence $\pi=(3,4,2)/9$. Both views reproduce this result,
with the exact values inside the confidence intervals. This is expected, since
the two views describe the same process: the minimum of independent
exponentials with rates $\lambda_{ij}$ is exponential with rate $q_i$, and
the winning timer is $j$ with probability $\lambda_{ij}/q_i$. Here all states
have the same exit rate $q_i=2$ (mean holding time 0.5), so the fraction of time
in each state equals the fraction of visits to it. State~2 is the most
frequent because it is the only exit of state~1 and is reachable from both
other states, while state~3 is entered only from state~2, and only half of the
time.

\section{C. Queuing systems}\label{sec:C}

\subsection{Exercise 6: Stability of the M/M/1 queue}

\textbf{Approach:} We simulated an M/M/1 queue and measured the average queuing
delay over the first \texttt{NumQueueCompleted} customers served

Student-$t$ confidence intervals. No warm-up was used since the purpose
is to observe the transient growth.

confidence intervals are narrow, but the estimates are not long-run averages,
since they just reflect the run length. Therefore steady-state performance
measures only make sense for $\rho<1$.

\textbf{Impact of the service-time distribution:} By the Pollaczek--Khinchine
formula, $W_q=\dfrac{\rho S(1+c_S^2)}{2(1-\rho)}$, where $c_S^2$ is the squared
coefficient of variation of the service time: $c_S^2=1$ for exponential and
$0$ for fixed sizes. Hence the M/D/1 queuing delay is exactly half of the M/M/1
one. The average delay depends on the variability of the service time and not
only on its mean, and the effect grows as $\rho\to1$.

\subsubsection{(c) Strict priority scheduling}

\textbf{Approach:} Packets are never interrupted, so this is a non-preemptive
M/D/1 queue with priorities. Cobham's formula gives, for class $k$,
\[
W_k = S + \frac{W_0}{(1-\sigma_{k-1})(1-\sigma_k)}, \qquad
W_0=\sum_i\frac{\lambda_i S^2}{2}, \qquad \sigma_k=\sum_{i\le k}\rho_i .
\]
With $\lambda_1=10$ (priority 1) and $\lambda_2=85$ pkt/s (priority 2),
$\rho=0.95$ and $W_0=4.75$~ms, so $W_1=15.28$~ms and $W_2=115.56$~ms, a ratio of
7.6. The default rates (10 and 80) give a ratio of exactly 4.0, too close to the
required limit of 4.

    \label{tab:ppl-prio}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc|ccc}
        Flow & Priority & $\lambda$ (pkt/s) & Theory (ms) & Simulation (ms) & Rel.\ err.\ (\%) \\
        \hline
        1 & 1 & 10 & 15.28  & $15.28\pm0.01$  & $+0.0$ \\
        2 & 2 & 85 & 115.56 & $115.02\pm1.72$ & $-0.5$ \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Conclusion:} The theoretical values lie inside the confidence intervals
of both flows. Priorities separate the two flows strongly: the simulated delay
of flow~2 is 7.5 times that of flow~1 (theory: 7.6), well above the required
factor of 4. The high-priority flow only waits for the packet in transmission and
for packets of its own class, so its delay is almost deterministic (confidence
interval of $\pm0.01$~ms), while the low-priority flow absorbs the load and the
variability. A FIFO queue at the same load would give both flows 105~ms, and the
$\lambda$-weighted mean of the priority delays,
$(10\cdot15.28+85\cdot115.56)/95=105$~ms, is the same: priorities redistribute
the delay between flows but do not reduce it.

\section{D. Workloads}\label{sec:D}

\subsection{Exercise 8: Workload variability in M/G/1 queues}

\textbf{Objective:} Study how the variability of the service time (job size)
affects (i) the average queuing delay of a single-server queue and (ii) the
precision of its simulation estimates, and find a way of reducing the delay.

\textbf{Setup:} Three systems with the same load ($\rho=0.5$) and the same
mean service time ($E[S]=2$, hence $\lambda=0.25$) are compared: M/M/1 and the
two M/G/1 systems with bimodal (mice--elephant) service times given in the

the variability of the service time, measured by its squared coefficient of
variation $c_S^2=E[S^2]/E[S]^2-1$ (Table~\ref{tab:wl-systems}). The theoretical

W_q=\frac{\lambda E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)}=\frac{1+c_S^2}{2}\cdot\frac{\rho\,E[S]}{1-\rho}.
\]
The simulations use \texttt{mg1\_bimodal.R}: each system was simulated in 100
independent runs of $10^5$ completed services, and the 95\% Student-$t$
confidence intervals were computed from the 100 run averages.

\subsubsection{(c) Priority for mice}

\textbf{Approach:} Mice and elephants are treated differently by giving mice
non-preemptive priority over elephants: the server always picks a waiting mouse
first, but an elephant in service is never interrupted. This applies the
shortest-job-first principle, so short jobs no longer wait behind long ones.
The simulator was modified to keep two queues (mice and elephants) and to serve
the mice queue first; the same 100 runs of $10^5$ services were used. The
theoretical delays follow Cobham's formula for non-preemptive priorities:
\begin{gather*}
W_0=\frac{\lambda E[S^2]}{2},\qquad \sigma_m=\lambda p_1 S_1,\\
W_{q,m}=\frac{W_0}{1-\sigma_m},\qquad
W_{q,e}=\frac{W_0}{(1-\sigma_m)(1-\rho)},\qquad
W_q=p_1W_{q,m}+p_2W_{q,e}.
\end{gather*}

to within 0.5\%. Elephants wait longer than with FIFO (4.19 vs.\ 3.25 and 34.2
vs.\ 25.8), but relative to their service time the penalty is small: their
average time in the system grows by about 7\% in both systems, while that of
the mice drops by 27\% (Bimodal~1) and 32\% (Bimodal~2). Since mice are the vast
majority of the jobs, the average improves. The estimates also become more
stable: the sample variance drops from 0.005 to 0.0008 (Bimodal~1) and from
1.82 to 0.40 (Bimodal~2).

Even so, mice in Bimodal~2 still wait 17.1 on average, about $8.5\times$ the
M/M/1 value. This is a limitation of non-preemptive priority: 99\% of $W_0$
comes from the residual service of an elephant already in service, which a
mouse still has to wait for. With preemptive-resume priority, mice would only
wait for other mice, and their delay would drop to
$W_{q,m}=\lambda p_1 S_1^2/\big(2(1-\sigma_m)\big)\approx0.16$
(0.15 for Bimodal~1), at the cost of a further delay for the elephants
(theoretical value only; not simulated).

\section{E. Server farms}\label{sec:E}

\subsection{Exercise 9: Random vs.\ JSQ task assignment}

\textbf{Objective:} Compare the random and Join-the-Shortest-Queue (JSQ) task
assignment policies in a Web server farm with two servers, for several values of
the system load $\rho$.

\textbf{Simulator:} The script \texttt{webserverfarm.R} generates jobs with
Poisson arrivals (rate $\lambda$) and exponentially distributed sizes. Each
server is a \emph{processor-sharing} (PS) server, as is typical of Web servers:
when $n$ jobs are present, each one receives a fraction $1/n$ of the server
capacity. To implement this, every time a job arrives at or leaves a server,
the function \texttt{rescheduleDepartures()} computes how much work each job
of that server has done since the last event and recomputes its departure
time with the new number of jobs. The random policy sends each job to a server
chosen uniformly at random; JSQ sends it to the server with the fewest jobs,
breaking ties at random. Each policy is simulated until $10^5$ jobs have
completed, and the average delay in the system (waiting plus service) is
computed. The script draws the job size, in service units, with rate
\texttt{ServiceRate} and serves it at speed \texttt{ServiceRate}, so the mean
service time is only $1/\mu$ when $\mu=1$; this is why the guide requires
$\mu=1$.

\textbf{Parameters:} $\mu=1$ (\texttt{ServiceRate}), $\lambda=2\rho\mu$
(\texttt{ArrivalRate}) for $\rho=0.3$, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95, and
$10^5$ completed jobs per run (\texttt{StoppingCondition}). Our script
\texttt{E9\_run.R} runs the course script unchanged, setting only these three
input lines and the seed, with 10 independent runs (seeds 1 to 10) per value of
$\rho$; 95\% Student-$t$ confidence intervals were computed from the 10 run
averages.

\textbf{Theory:} With random assignment, the Poisson arrival stream is split
into two independent Poisson streams of rate $\lambda/2$, so each server is an
M/M/1-PS queue. Its average delay is the same as that of an M/M/1 FIFO queue:
\[
T_{\mathrm{random}}=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda/2}=\frac{1}{\mu(1-\rho)},
\]
which is the value computed by the script (\texttt{TrueAvgDelay}). JSQ makes
the two servers dependent on each other and has no simple closed-form
solution, so it is evaluated by simulation only.

\textbf{Results:} The random policy agrees with the theory at all loads, with
the theoretical value inside the confidence interval. JSQ is always better,
and its advantage grows with the load: the average delay is 20\% lower than
with random assignment at $\rho=0.3$, 40\% lower at $\rho=0.8$ and 45\% lower at
$\rho=0.95$ (Table~\ref{tab:wsf} and Figure~\ref{fig:wsf}). In absolute terms,
the difference grows from 0.28 to 8.7 mean service times.

\textbf{Conclusion:} Random assignment ignores the state of the servers, so the
two queues evolve independently: one server can accumulate several jobs while
the other is idle, and part of the capacity of the farm is wasted. JSQ sends
each job to the less loaded server, which keeps both servers busy whenever
there are at least two jobs, and with processor sharing a new job also shares
its server with fewer other jobs. The gain grows with the load because the cost
of an imbalance grows with it. At low load, jobs rarely overlap, so it seldom
matters which server is chosen. At high load, the servers are almost always
busy, imbalances are frequent, and each one is expensive, since the delay grows
sharply as $\rho\to1$. Using the state of the servers therefore matters most
precisely when the system is busiest. JSQ only
requires the dispatcher to know the number of jobs at each server, which is
easy to track at a front-end load balancer (Exercise~10). The confidence
intervals widen with the load (from 0.4\% to 6--9\% of the mean), as in the
previous exercises; the random estimate at $\rho=0.95$ is 3.6\% below theory,
but still inside its interval, which is consistent with each run starting
from an empty system and taking a long time to reach steady state at such a
high load.

\subsection{Exercise 10: Load balancing in NGINX}

\textbf{Objective:} Describe what NGINX is and identify which of its
load-balancing algorithms give the lowest average response delay.

\textbf{What is NGINX:} NGINX is a software-based load balancer and
application delivery controller, available as NGINX Open Source and as the
commercial NGINX Plus, and used by more than 350 million websites~\cite{f5-lb}.
A load balancer acts as a proxy between the users and a group of servers and
distributes the incoming requests among them, so that the servers are used
evenly, which improves the reliability, availability and performance of the
application~\cite{f5-lb}.

\textbf{Load-balancing algorithms:} Load balancing can be \emph{static}, based
on fixed information about the servers and suited to predictable traffic, or
\emph{dynamic}, deciding at runtime where to send each request and suited to
traffic with high fluctuations~\cite{f5-lb}. The algorithms described in the
reference are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:nginx}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering \small
    \caption{Load-balancing algorithms~\cite{f5-lb}.}
    \label{tab:nginx}
    \begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.6cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{11.4cm}}
        \hline
        Algorithm & How the server is chosen \\
        \hline
        Round Robin & Servers are used in turn, in a fixed rotation. \\
        Threshold & Requests are distributed according to a threshold set by the administrator. \\
        Least Connections & Server with the fewest active connections, taking its relative capacity into account. \\
        Least Time & Combination of the fastest response time and the fewest active connections. \\
        Random with Two Choices & Two servers are picked at random, and Least Connections or Least Time chooses between them. \\
        URL Hash / Source IP Hash & A hash of the requested URL, or of the client's IP address, always maps the same request or client to the same server. \\
        Consistent Hashing & Clients and servers are placed on a ring, and each request goes to the next server on the ring. \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Which algorithms perform better:} The algorithms that use the current
state of the servers give the lowest average response delay. Least Time is the
best, since it is the only one that directly uses the response time of the
servers, followed by Least Connections and Random with Two Choices, which use
the number of active connections. Least Connections is the JSQ policy of
Exercise~9: in a web server, the number of active connections is the number of
requests sharing the server, and JSQ reduced the average delay of random
assignment by 20 to 45\%, with the largest gains at high load
(Table~\ref{tab:wsf}). Static algorithms, such as Round Robin and the hash
methods, ignore how busy each server is, so a server can keep receiving
requests while another one is idle. The hash methods are mainly used to keep a
client on the same server (session persistence), not to reduce the delay.

\textbf{Objective:} Develop a script that, for any network topology and any
set of flows with fixed routing, calculates with the Kleinrock approximation
(i) the average packet delay of each flow, (ii) the average number of packets
in the network, and (iii) the average packet delay in the network, using the
same data structures as the \texttt{pnet.R} simulator.

\textbf{Theory:} In a packet switched network, a packet waits in a queue and
is then transmitted on every link of its route, so its delay is the sum of the
delays on those links. With Poisson arrivals and exponentially distributed
packet sizes, the first link of a flow is an M/M/1 queue, but the following
links are not: a packet keeps its length from link to link, so the arrivals at
a downstream link are correlated with its service times. The Kleinrock
approximation ignores this correlation and models every link $k$ as an
independent M/M/1 queue, with arrival rate $\lambda_k$ (the sum of the rates
of the flows that cross the link) and service rate $\mu_k=C_k/\bar{L}$, where
$C_k$ is the link capacity and $\bar{L}$ the mean packet size. It requires
$\rho_k=\lambda_k/\mu_k<1$ on every link. With $L$ the average number of
packets and $W$ the average delay:
\begin{align*}
    L_k &= \frac{\lambda_k}{\mu_k-\lambda_k}, &
    W_k &= \frac{1}{\mu_k-\lambda_k}, \\
    \text{(i)}\;\; W_f &= \sum_{k\,\in\,\text{route}(f)} W_k, &
    \text{(ii)}\;\; L &= \sum_k L_k, \qquad
    \text{(iii)}\;\; W = \frac{L}{\gamma},\;\; \gamma=\sum_f \lambda_f ,
\end{align*}
where (iii) is Little's law applied to the whole network. Note that $\gamma$
is the total rate offered to the network, not $\sum_k\lambda_k$, which counts
a packet once per link it crosses.

\textbf{Implementation:} The script \texttt{E11\_Kleinrock.R} is connected to
the course simulator. The network is defined only in the course file \texttt{parameters.R} of
\texttt{scripts\_helper/pnet} (the \texttt{LinkCapacities} vector and the
\texttt{Flows} list, with links numbered from 1), so studying another network
only requires editing that file. The script:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item reads \texttt{parameters.R}, as \texttt{pnet.R} does;
    \item computes the Kleinrock approximation in the function
    \texttt{kleinrock()}: it adds the rate of each flow to every link of its
    route (the same idea as \texttt{loadlinks()} in \texttt{bg.R}), stops if any
    link has $\rho_k\ge1$, and computes $L_k$ (with the same expression used in
    \texttt{bg.R}), $W_k$, and (i), (ii) and (iii);
    \item runs \texttt{pnet.R}, unchanged, 10 times with different seeds, and
    takes the average delay of each flow from its \texttt{FlowStats} variable;
    \item compares the two, with 95\% Student-$t$ confidence intervals for the
    simulation; the simulated $L$ is obtained with Little's law, $L=\gamma W$.
\end{enumerate}
The script can also be run by other scripts on a different copy of the
simulator folder, which is how it is reused in Exercises~12 and~13.

\textbf{Test:} The script was tested with the example of the lectures (pktnet
slides 14--15), which is also the default network of \texttt{pnet.R}: links
$\ell_1$ ($1\!\to\!3$) and $\ell_2$ ($2\!\to\!3$) with 100~Mb/s, link $\ell_3$
($3\!\to\!4$) with 200~Mb/s, $\bar{L}=1000$~bytes, and three flows: 7.5~kpkt/s

By hand, $\mu_1=\mu_2=10^8/8000=12\,500$~pkt/s and $\mu_3=25\,000$~pkt/s;

\subsection{Exercise 12: Accuracy of the Kleinrock approximation}

\textbf{Objective:} For a network of two tandem links of 64~kb/s, with a single
flow from the first to the last node (Poisson arrivals, exponential packet
sizes with mean 1000~bits), compare the average packet delay obtained with the
Kleinrock approximation and with the \texttt{pnet.R} simulator, for
$\rho=\lambda/\mu=0.05$, 0.5, 0.95 and 0.975.

\textbf{Theory:} Each link has $\mu=64\,000/1000=64$~packets/s and
$\lambda=\rho\mu$. With the Kleinrock approximation both links are independent
M/M/1 queues, so
\[
W=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}+\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}=\frac{2}{\mu(1-\rho)} .
\]

\textbf{Parameters:} The script \texttt{E12\_KleinrockTandem.R} reuses
\texttt{E11\_Kleinrock.R}. For each $\rho$ it only writes the
\texttt{parameters.R} of the network, in a temporary copy of the simulator
folder (the course files are not modified), and runs
\texttt{E11\_Kleinrock.R} on it, which computes the approximation and runs
\texttt{pnet.R} 10 times. The \texttt{parameters.R} for $\rho=0.975$ is:
\begin{configbox}
parameters <- function() {
  LinkCapacities<<-c(64e3,64e3) #In bits/sec
  Flows<<-list(list(rate=62.4,packetsize=1000,route=c(1,2))) #lambda = rho*mu
  endTime<<-1e+06*(1/62.4) #About 1e+06 packets
}
\end{configbox}
The other values of $\rho$ only change \texttt{rate} and \texttt{endTime}. The
simulation time was chosen to give about $10^5$ packets per run for
$\rho=0.05$ and 0.5 ($\texttt{endTime}=31\,250$ and 3125~s), $5\times10^5$ for
$\rho=0.95$ (8224~s) and $10^6$ for $\rho=0.975$ (16\,026~s), because the
variance of the delay grows quickly as $\rho\to1$.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Average packet delay in two tandem links of 64~kb/s (simulation: mean of 10 runs $\pm$ 95\% CI half-width).}
    \label{tab:tandem}
    \begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c}
        $\rho$ & $\lambda$ (pkt/s) & Kleinrock & Simulation & Kleinrock error \\
        \hline
        0.05  & 3.2  & 32.89~ms  & $33.47\pm0.06$~ms & $-1.7\%$ \\
        0.5   & 32   & 62.50~ms  & $64.77\pm0.36$~ms & $-3.5\%$ \\
        0.95  & 60.8 & 625.0~ms  & $397.8\pm9.2$~ms  & $+57\%$ \\
        0.975 & 62.4 & 1250~ms   & $745.9\pm42.9$~ms & $+68\%$ \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Results:} At low and medium load ($\rho\le0.5$) the approximation is
close to the simulation, with errors of a few percent, although the theoretical
value is still outside the confidence interval and slightly below it. At high
load the approximation fails: it overestimates the delay by 57\% at
$\rho=0.95$ and by 68\% at $\rho=0.975$ (Table~\ref{tab:tandem}).

\textbf{Conclusion:} The error comes from the independence assumption. In
\texttt{pnet.R}, as in a real network, a packet keeps its length on both links,
so its transmission time is the same on the two links, and the arrivals at the
second link are correlated with its service times: two consecutive packets
arrive at the second link separated by at least the transmission time of the
second one. At high load, almost all the queueing happens at the first link,
which delivers the packets to the second link already spaced at the rate the
second link can serve them, so the second link has almost no queue. The real
delay is close to that of one M/M/1 queue plus one transmission time (for
$\rho=0.95$, $1/(64\times0.05)+1/64=328$~ms, plus some extra waiting), while
the approximation counts two full M/M/1 queues (625~ms). At low load the effect
is small and in the opposite direction: packets that queued together at the
first link leave it back to back, and when a long packet precedes a short one,
the short one waits again at the second link. The Kleinrock approximation is
therefore accurate at low and medium load, and in general when many flows are
merged and split along the routes, which dilutes the correlation. A single
flow over links of equal capacity at high load, as here, is its worst case.

\subsection{Exercise 13: Routing in a packet switched network}

\textbf{Objective:} For the network of Figure~\ref{fig:pnet13}, with all links
of 256~kb/s and four flows with fixed routing, (a) calculate the average
network packet delay with the Kleinrock approximation, (b) confirm it by
simulation, (c) find the optimal bifurcation of flow 1 by hand, and (d) check
with the gradient projection algorithm (\texttt{bg.R}) whether bifurcating all
flows improves the delay further.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{../images/E13_network.png}
    \caption{Network of Exercise 13, with the link numbering used in \texttt{pnet.R}.}
    \label{fig:pnet13}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Setup:} With $\bar{L}=1000$~bits, every link has $\mu=256$~packets/s.
The links are numbered as in Figure~\ref{fig:pnet13}, and the flows are:
flow 1, route 1-2-4-6 ($\ell_1,\ell_3,\ell_6$), 215~pkt/s; flow 2, route 1-3-5
($\ell_2,\ell_5$), 64~pkt/s; flow 3, route 1-3-5-6 ($\ell_2,\ell_5,\ell_7$),
128~pkt/s; and flow 4, route 2-5 ($\ell_4$), 128~pkt/s. The total offered rate
is $\gamma=535$~pkt/s. The script \texttt{E13\_PacketNetwork.R} solves all the
questions: it reuses \texttt{E11\_Kleinrock.R} for the Kleinrock approximation
and the \texttt{pnet.R} simulations, and runs \texttt{bg.R} unchanged.

\textbf{a) Kleinrock approximation:} The link loads are
$\lambda_1=\lambda_3=\lambda_6=215$, $\lambda_2=\lambda_5=64+128=192$ and
$\lambda_4=\lambda_7=128$~pkt/s. Then $L_k=215/41=5.244$ on $\ell_1,\ell_3,\ell_6$,
$192/64=3$ on $\ell_2,\ell_5$ and $128/128=1$ on $\ell_4,\ell_7$, so
\[
L=3\times5.244+2\times3+2\times1=23.73~\text{packets}, \qquad
W=\frac{L}{\gamma}=\frac{23.73}{535}=44.36~\text{ms}.
\]
The flow delays are $W_1=3/41=73.2$~ms, $W_2=2/64=31.3$~ms,
$W_3=2/64+1/128=39.1$~ms and $W_4=1/128=7.8$~ms.

\textbf{b) Simulation:} \texttt{pnet.R} was run 10 times with the following
\texttt{parameters.R} (about 53\,500 packets per run):
\begin{configbox}
parameters <- function() {

  Flows<<-list(list(rate=215,packetsize=1000,route=c(1,3,6)),
               list(rate=64,packetsize=1000,route=c(2,5)),
               list(rate=128,packetsize=1000,route=c(2,5,7)),

  endTime<<-100 #In seconds
}
\end{configbox}

The simulation confirms the order of magnitude of the delay, but the
approximation overestimates the network delay by 21\%
(Table~\ref{tab:pnet13ab}). As in Exercise~12, the error is mostly in flow 1,
which is the only flow on links $\ell_3$ and $\ell_6$ at a high load
($\rho=0.84$): there, flow 1 behaves like the single flow over tandem links of
Exercise~12. Flow 4, which crosses a single link, agrees with the theory, as
expected for an M/M/1 queue.

\textbf{c) Optimal bifurcation of flow 1:} Let $x$ be the rate of flow 1 on
route 1-2-4-6 and $215-x$ its rate on 1-2-5-6. Links $\ell_3$ and $\ell_6$ carry
$x$; links $\ell_4$ and $\ell_7$ carry $128+(215-x)=343-x$ (flow 4 is on $\ell_4$
and flow 3 on $\ell_7$); $\ell_1$ carries 215 in both cases, and the other links
are not affected. The optimal bifurcation minimizes $L$, and is obtained when
both routes have the same first derivative length, $\sum_k \partial L_k/\partial
\lambda_k$ with $\partial L_k/\partial\lambda_k=\mu/(\mu-\lambda_k)^2$ (link
$\ell_1$ is common to both routes and cancels out):
\[
\frac{2\mu}{(\mu-x)^2}=\frac{2\mu}{\big(\mu-(343-x)\big)^2}
\;\Rightarrow\; x=343-x \;\Rightarrow\; x=171.5~\text{pkt/s},
\]
so 171.5~pkt/s use 1-2-4-6 and 43.5~pkt/s use 1-2-5-6. The four links
$\ell_3,\ell_4,\ell_6,\ell_7$ then carry 171.5~pkt/s each, with
$L_k=171.5/84.5=2.030$, so
\[
L=5.244+2\times3+4\times2.030=19.36~\text{packets}, \qquad
W=\frac{19.36}{535}=36.19~\text{ms},
\]
18\% lower than the 44.36~ms of question a). The script checks this result by
minimizing $W(x)$ numerically with the \texttt{kleinrock()} function of
Exercise~11, which also gives $x=171.5$~pkt/s (Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation}),
and by simulating the bifurcated network (flow 1 split into two flows of 171.5
and 43.5~pkt/s), which gives $W=33.88\pm0.82$~ms.

\section{Conclusion}

The exercises of this laboratory show how Markov models and queuing theory can predict the performance of networks and systems, and how discrete-event simulation can confirm those predictions. In all cases where an exact model exists, the simulation estimates agree with the theory to within a few percent, and in most cases the theoretical value lies inside the confidence interval.

Discrete-time Markov chains extend independent Bernoulli models by capturing temporal dependence, and their transition probabilities can be estimated directly from an observed trace. Applied to slotted ALOHA, a DTMC model shows that throughput is limited by collisions and collapses when the offered load is too high, which is why carrier-sensing protocols are preferred whenever propagation delay is small. The limiting probabilities of DTMCs and CTMCs can be obtained equivalently from the balance equations, from matrix powers, and by simulation, and the two views of a CTMC lead to the same process.

The queuing exercises show that steady-state performance measures only exist for $\rho<1$; for $\rho\ge1$, the measured delay simply grows with the length of the run. For a stable queue, delay depends not only on the load and the mean service time, but also on the variability of the service time: fixed packet sizes halve the M/M/1 queuing delay, while highly variable (mice--elephant) workloads increase it by more than an order of magnitude and make simulation estimates much less precise. Scheduling can redistribute this delay: strict priorities protect a high-priority flow without reducing the average delay, while giving priority to short jobs reduces the average delay by about 30\%.

Finally, in a server farm, how jobs are assigned to servers matters as much as the capacity of the servers. Assigning jobs at random wastes capacity, because one server can be overloaded while another is idle. Using the state of the servers, as Join the Shortest Queue does, reduced the average delay by up to 45\%, with the largest gains at the highest loads. This is the principle behind the Least Connections and Least Time methods of load balancers such as NGINX.

In packet switched networks, the Kleinrock approximation gives the delay of any network from simple M/M/1 formulas, but it ignores that a packet keeps its length from link to link. It is accurate at low and medium load and when many flows share the links, but it can overestimate the delay by more than 50\% for a single flow over tandem links at high load. Even so, it is a good basis for routing decisions: balancing the load by bifurcating the flows over several routes reduced the average network delay by about 19\%, and both the hand optimization and the gradient projection algorithm were confirmed by simulation.
